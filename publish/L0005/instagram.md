## Caption

Always buy 100 shares? Nice round number. With a $4 stop that's $400 on the line, not $100.

A fixed share count lets the stop decide your risk. Flip it: decide the risk first, then let the stop decide the size.

The math on a $10,000 account risking 1% ($100):
Stop $1 away: 100 shares
Stop $2 away: 50 shares
Stop $4 away: 25 shares
Shares = risk ÷ stop distance. Round down.

Full size on a wide stop: that's the scary one. Size down and it's still $100.

Your 100-share habit can retire.

## Alt text

Animated seesaw on a dark blue grid. On the left arm sits an amber block labelled $100, the risk budget, 1% of $10,000. On the right arm, a stack of white blocks (100 shares) sits on a ruler marked $1 to $4 of stop distance. As the stack slides out to $2 and $4 with the same 100 shares, the beam tips and the at-risk number turns red: $200, then $400. Then the sizing is flipped: at $2 half the blocks fly off, leaving 50 shares, and at $4 only 25 shares remain; each time the beam wobbles and levels at $100 at risk. Cards follow: shares equal risk divided by stop distance, $100 divided by $4 is 25 shares, round down; the myth 'a wide stop means big risk' struck out; and the rule: pick the risk, the stop picks the size.
